1. Process States: As process executes, it changes state. Each process may be in one of the following
states.
a. New: OS is about to pick the program & convert it into process. OR the process is being
created.
b. Run: Instructions are being executed; CPU is allocated.
c. Waiting: Waiting for IO.
d. Ready: The process is in memory, waiting to be assigned to a processor.
e. Terminated: The process has finished execution. PCB entry removed from process table.

2. Process Queues:

A process queue is a collection of processes waiting in a specific state (new, ready, or waiting) managed by the operating system.

a. Job Queue:
i. Processes in new state.
ii. Present in secondary memory.
iii. Job Schedular (Long term schedular (LTS)) picks process from the pool and
loads them into memory for execution.

b. Ready Queue:
i. Processes in Ready state.
ii. Present in main memory.
iii. CPU Schedular (Short-term schedular) picks process from ready queue and
dispatch it to CPU.

c. Waiting Queue:
i. Processes in Wait state.

A Long-Term Scheduler (LTS) is the scheduler that selects processes from the job queue (in secondary storage) and loads them into main memory for execution.

It is called long-term because it makes scheduling decisions less frequently (compared to short-term schedulers) and controls the degree of multiprogramming in the system.

The Job Scheduler is called the Long-Term Scheduler because it works at a longer time interval — it decides which jobs (processes) from the job pool in secondary storage should be admitted into main memory for execution.

It is "long-term" since its decisions are less frequent (compared to CPU scheduling), and it controls the degree of multiprogramming over a long duration, balancing I/O-bound and CPU-bound processes.

A Short-Term Scheduler (STS) is the scheduler that selects a process from the ready queue in main memory and allocates the CPU to it.

It is called short-term because it makes scheduling decisions very frequently (in milliseconds) to ensure smooth CPU utilization and fast process switching.

CPU scheduling is called Short-Term Scheduling because it selects which process from the ready queue in main memory will get the CPU next, and these decisions happen very frequently (in milliseconds).

It is called short-term since it operates over short intervals of time, making rapid and frequent scheduling decisions to keep the CPU busy and ensure efficient process execution.


The degree of multiprogramming is defined as the number of processes that are present in main memory and can compete for CPU execution at the same time.
The degree of multiprogramming is controlled by the Long-Term Scheduler (Job Scheduler).

👉 It decides how many processes should be admitted into the system (main memory) from the job pool, thereby directly controlling the degree of multiprogramming.

A dispatcher is a program module in the operating system that gives control of the CPU to the process selected by the short-term scheduler, by performing context switching, switching to user mode, and jumping to the proper program location to restart execution.
